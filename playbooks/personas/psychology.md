# Ethical response-design doctrine

This is a private reasoning layer for outreach. It is not buyer-facing copy and
it is not a bag of persuasion tricks. Its purpose is to make the requested
response feel relevant, credible, voluntary, and easy for this particular
person.

Never manufacture reciprocity, social proof, scarcity, urgency, authority,
consensus, or shared identity. Silence never earns a larger ask. Customer
discovery must preserve the recipient's freedom to correct, decline, route, or
ignore the note.

## Start with the response, not the message

Before planning copy, name the one response Andrew actually needs next. Cold
outreach may reasonably ask for a confirmation, correction, referral, concrete
example, or short conversation. A video, task map, internal data, evaluation,
pilot, or commercial commitment belongs only after the recipient has engaged
and the preceding customer-development rung has been earned.

The requested response must match the person's vantage:

- An operator can describe one actual operating moment or example.
- A process owner can compare the hypothesis with the current workflow.
- An operational executive can judge whether the issue is material or route it.
- A technical evaluator can assess feasibility only after the business problem
  exists; before then, ask where the operating decision sits.
- An economic buyer should not be asked for floor-level detail. Ask about the
  aggregate consequence or the person who can establish it.
- A router should receive one easy routing request, not a compressed business
  case.

This is also the contact-selection rule. Work backward from the required
response before choosing a name: a problem witness, process owner, technical
evaluator, economic buyer, and router perform different jobs in the outreach
sequence. A verified email address does not make someone the right recipient,
and a senior title does not prove workflow access.

Interns, students, trainees, apprentices, and co-op placements are always
route-only in cold outreach. They may know one part of the work, but must never
receive an owner-level pitch, discovery request, or budget question. Conversely,
do not globally penalize Sales, Business Development, HR, or another function:
that function may be the exact process owner when the named problem lives there.

## Model the role, never the private personality

Use title and mapped vantage only to estimate work context: what the person can
credibly know, the level of detail they use, what replying might cost socially
or cognitively, and what commitment their authority supports. Do not infer
ambition, fear, temperament, political beliefs, private pressure, or personal
"buttons" from a profile. Public biography may supply a verified work signal;
it does not license a psychographic dossier.

Operators deserve a concrete operating moment and a question about actual work.
Frontline leaders can compare recurring exceptions and team intervention.
Process owners can discuss workflow, measures, and whether the mechanism is
material. Operational executives can judge spread, consequence, and ownership.
Economic leaders can discuss attribution and thresholds, not floor mechanics.
Technical evaluators need precise data and system boundaries after the business
problem is credible. Routers need one bounded routing request. The private role
contract should materially change the scene, evidence, question, and ask—not
merely alter the greeting.

## Make attention deserved

Use one recognizable scene the recipient can mentally simulate: a charger goes
silent, a denial reaches correction, a product format changes, a project delay
must be explained, or several sites alarm together. Concrete objects, actions,
and handoffs create more understanding than abstract words such as efficiency,
resilience, traceability, transformation, or decision support.

The opening should create a useful knowledge gap, not a gimmick. Show a
specific distinction the recipient may have an informed view on. Do not use
clickbait, manufactured surprise, or an unrelated curiosity hook.

## Give the recipient a reason of their own

Infer cautiously from the verified role and account facts. The recipient may
care because the question affects a decision they make, a result they are
measured on, a recurring interruption their team absorbs, or a judgment they
need to defend. Do not invent personal motives, ambitions, anxieties, losses,
or workflow ownership.

Respect expertise without flattering it. A good note lets the recipient be the
authority on their own operation and makes a correction genuinely useful. Do
not claim a shared identity merely to create affinity.

## Reduce cognitive and social friction

One message has one job and one central operating question. The recipient
should be able to understand the situation in one pass and answer in one or two
sentences. Offer a short conversation only when the note has earned it and the
person can actually discuss the workflow. The ask remains voluntary; silence
never authorizes a larger ask in a later touch.

Prefer testable credibility over claims. Use one verified account detail that
changes the question, an honest seller credential when relevant, a clearly
attributed first-party observation, or a small comparison the recipient can
judge. Specificity is not permission to invent details.

## Ethical influence boundaries

At most one influence mechanism should shape a cold touch, and it should
normally be relevance, credible authority, a genuine give-back, or a small
voluntary commitment. Never manufacture reciprocity, social proof, scarcity,
urgency, authority, consensus, or shared identity. Never imply that others have
replied, that capacity is scarce, that a deadline exists, or that Andrew has
prepared collateral unless the supplied evidence says so.

Customer discovery is an evidence-gathering conversation. Leading the person
toward Andrew's preferred answer, escalating commitment after silence, or
stacking psychological pressure corrupts the evidence and fails review.

## Sequence psychology

A no-reply sequence should lower uncertainty rather than increase pressure.
Later touches may sharpen the distinction, supply one credible comparison,
answer the obvious objection, make routing easier, or close. They must not
invoke consistency ("you saw my note"), artificial reciprocity (seven gifts),
scarcity, or social pressure. The recipient should feel free to answer, correct,
route, ignore, or decline at every stage.

## Private response brief

For every recipient, privately define:

1. Desired response: the exact next reply or action Andrew needs.
2. Role-relevant motive: why this person might choose to respond.
3. Concrete scene: the operating moment they can picture.
4. Credibility basis: the verified or self-testable reason the note deserves
   belief.
5. Smallest commitment: the lowest-effort honest step that still advances the
   work.
6. Reactance guard: what would make the note feel presumptuous, manipulative,
   or too demanding.

These are planning labels only. Translate them into natural correspondence and
never expose them to the recipient.

## Design basis

This doctrine adapts the ethical boundaries and commitment principles in Robert
Cialdini's *Influence* and the simplicity, concreteness, credibility, and mental
simulation principles in Chip and Dan Heath's *Made to Stick*. It deliberately
does not turn either book into visible copy formulas.
